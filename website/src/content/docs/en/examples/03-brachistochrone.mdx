<p class="lead">
  Release a ball from rest on a frictionless track. What shape reaches a point 1 m horizontally away fastest?
  A straight line is shortest, but it is not fastest. Johann Bernoulli posed this classical problem in 1696.
</p>

## Situation

Rather than searching directly for a track, choose the ball's **direction at each instant**.
A steep descent builds speed but makes little horizontal progress; a shallow track advances horizontally but accelerates slowly.
The fastest path balances these effects by descending steeply first, then turning horizontally.

Only terminal horizontal position is fixed. Depth is free, so the target is a vertical line.
Gravity and horizontal distance are both set to 1.

## Formulation

### Dynamics

Depth is positive **downward**. Angle $\theta=0$ points down, and $\theta=90^\circ$ is horizontal.

$$
\dot{x}=v\sin\theta,\qquad\dot{y}=v\cos\theta,\qquad\dot{v}=g\cos\theta.
$$

The first two equations resolve velocity into components. Only the component of gravity along the track accelerates the ball.
Since $v\dot v=g\dot y$, energy is conserved:

$$
\tfrac12v^2=gy,\qquad v=\sqrt{2gy}.
$$

### Boundary conditions

$$
x(0)=y(0)=v(0)=0,\qquad x(t_f)=l.
$$

Terminal depth and speed are left to optimization.

### Constraints

$$
y\ge0,\qquad\theta\ge0,\qquad t_f\ge0.1.
$$

The depth inequality excludes the initial node, already fixed by an equality.
The angle condition restricts the intended forward-moving solution, and the time bound prevents a degenerate duration.
Inequalities are written as nonnegative residuals.

### Objective and parameters

$$
J=t_f.
$$

| Symbol | Parameter | Value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $g$ | `PARAMETERS["g"]` | 1 m/s² |
| $l$ | `PARAMETERS["l"]` | 1 m |

### Initial guess

Use time 0 to 2, horizontal position linear from 0 to 1, depth linear from 0 to $1/\sqrt3$, and constant direction $30^\circ$.
Speed starts from zero at all nodes. The guess is not dynamically consistent; optimization corrects it.

## Explanation

### The cycloid

The fastest descent curve is a **cycloid**, traced by a point on a circle rolling beneath a horizontal ceiling:

$$
x=a(\phi-\sin\phi),\qquad y=a(1-\cos\phi).
$$

It points down at $\phi=0$ and becomes horizontal at its deepest point, $\phi=\pi$.
An optical analogy explains the shape: Snell's law for a minimum-time path requires $\sin\theta/v$ to remain constant.
Since $v=\sqrt{2gy}$, the required curve has $\sin\theta/\sqrt y$ constant, which gives the cycloid.

### Why finish horizontally?

Terminal depth is free, so a downward velocity component at arrival contributes nothing to reaching the target line sooner.
The best endpoint directs all speed horizontally. Thus $a\pi=l$ and

$$
y(t_f)=\frac{2l}{\pi},\qquad t_f=\sqrt{\frac{\pi l}{g}}.
$$

For $g=l=1$, $t_f\approx1.7725$. Along the cycloid, $\theta=\phi/2$ and $\phi$ grows linearly with time,
so the angle history is a straight line.

### Numerical accuracy

The dynamics are nonlinear because of trigonometric terms and products of states and controls.
The smooth solution nevertheless gives about seven-digit agreement with 20 nodes
([convergence plot](../../theory/pseudospectral/#checking-accuracy)).

## Try it

- Set `l` to 2: duration becomes $\sqrt{2\pi}\approx2.507$ s and final depth $4/\pi\approx1.273$ m.
- Set `g` to 4: the track is unchanged, but duration halves to about 0.886 s.
- Reduce 20 nodes to 8 or 7 and compare errors (roughly $10^{-7}$ and $10^{-5}$). With 5 or 6 nodes, success can be reported despite errors exceeding 0.2 s. Solver success and accuracy are different checks.
