<p class="lead">
  How should a rocket vary its thrust to reach the greatest altitude with a fixed amount of propellant?
  This classical optimal control problem was posed by R. H. Goddard.
</p>

## Situation

Burning propellant makes the vehicle lighter, increasing acceleration for the same thrust.
Flying faster increases drag quadratically, but climbing higher reduces atmospheric density.
Maximum thrust from the start wastes energy against drag; very gentle thrust prolongs gravity losses.
The optimum balances both losses with a time-varying thrust.

All quantities are **dimensionless**, with Earth radius, surface gravity, and initial mass normalized to 1.
Here $h$ is measured from the planet's center; height above the surface is $h-1$.

## Formulation

### Dynamics

$$
\dot h=v,\qquad\dot v=\frac{T-D(h,v)}m-g(h),\qquad\dot m=-\frac Tc.
$$

$$
D(h,v)=D_cv^2\exp\!\left(-H_c\frac{h-h_0}{h_0}\right),\qquad
 g(h)=g_0\left(\frac{h_0}h\right)^2.
$$

Drag scales with speed squared and decreases exponentially with altitude.
Gravity decreases with squared distance from the center.
Exhaust velocity $c$ determines propellant consumption: larger $c$ uses less mass for the same thrust.

### Boundary conditions

$$
h(0)=1,\quad v(0)=0,\quad m(0)=1,\qquad v(t_f)=0,\quad m(t_f)=0.6.
$$

Start at rest on the surface. Finish at the highest point, with zero velocity and all available propellant consumed.

### Constraints and objective

$$
0\le T\le3.5,\quad h\ge1,\quad v\ge0,\quad0.6\le m\le1,\qquad J=-h(t_f).
$$

Minimizing negative height maximizes height. Maximum thrust is 3.5 times initial weight.

### Parameters

| Symbol | Meaning | Value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $h_0,g_0,m_0$ | Initial radius, surface gravity, initial mass | 1 |
| $D_c$ | Drag coefficient factor | 310 |
| $H_c$ | Atmospheric decay factor | 500 |
| $c$ | Exhaust velocity | 0.5 |
| $T_{\max}$ | Maximum thrust | 3.5 |
| $m_f$ | Dry mass | 0.6 |

### Initial guess

Use final time 0.3, cubic height from 1 to 1.01, cubic mass from 1 to 0.6, zero velocity,
and a cubic thrust decrease from maximum to zero.

## Explanation

### Intermediate thrust: a singular arc

Maximum thrust initially builds speed against gravity. As drag grows, throttling back maintains a speed that balances drag and gravity losses.
This interval of thrust strictly between its bounds is a **singular arc**.
As the atmosphere thins, the rocket consumes the remaining propellant and coasts upward.

Unlike the bang-bang control in [Example 02](../02-move-stop/), the intermediate thrust must be determined by the physical balance.
It can be difficult to handle with [indirect methods](../../theory/methods/), but a direct formulation needs no special treatment.

### Thrust oscillations

The interpolated thrust oscillates and falls gradually near burnout, although the true optimum switches discontinuously to zero.
One polynomial through 50 nodes cannot represent that jump accurately
([multiple phases](../../theory/pseudospectral/#multiple-phases)).
[Example 08](../08-goddard-1knot/) splits near burnout to reduce the oscillation.

## Try it

- Change the node count from 50 to 30 or 80 and compare oscillations and final height.
- Reduce `PARAMETERS["Dc"]` and check whether the singular arc shortens and maximum-thrust operation lasts longer.
- Lower `T_max` and compare the time spent at the thrust limit.
