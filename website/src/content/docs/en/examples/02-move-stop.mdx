  Move a stationary cart 10 m and stop exactly at the destination. Acceleration is limited to ±2 m/s².
  When should you accelerate, and when should you brake, to arrive as soon as possible?

## Situation

[Example 01](../01-minimum-energy-transfer/) chose velocity directly. A real vehicle instead controls **acceleration**.
Velocity accumulates from acceleration and cannot change instantly, so both position and velocity are states.
Braking too early wastes time; braking too late overshoots the destination.

## Formulation

### Dynamics

$$
\dot{x}=v,\qquad\dot{v}=a.
$$

There is no friction or drag. The control affects position through two successive integrations.

### Boundary conditions

$$
x(0)=0,\quad v(0)=0,\qquad x(t_f)=10,\quad v(t_f)=0.
$$

The final time is unknown.

### Constraints

$$
-2\le a\le2.
$$

Positive acceleration speeds up the cart; negative acceleration brakes it. The same bounds apply in both phases.

### Phases

Split time at an unknown $t_1$. Position and velocity are continuous at the knot, but acceleration may jump.
The optimum has one acceleration switch, so the knot can represent it exactly.

### Objective and parameters

$$
J=t_f.
$$

| Symbol | Parameter | Value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $d$ | `PARAMETERS["distance"]` | 10 m |
| $a_{\max}$ | `PARAMETERS["max_acceleration"]` | 2 m/s² |

### Initial guess

Use phase times $0\to3\to6$ s, position linear from 0 to $d$, constant velocity $d/6$, and zero acceleration.
This does not satisfy the zero endpoint velocities. The solver adjusts times, states, and controls to satisfy them and shorten the motion.

## Explanation

### Why maximum acceleration and maximum braking?

The **area under the velocity curve is distance**, fixed at 10 m. We want that area with the smallest possible width in time.
The curve must start and finish at zero, and its slopes cannot exceed $\pm2$.
The narrowest possible shape is a triangle with the steepest allowed rise and fall.
A trapezoid or rounded curve needs more time for the same area.

A control that stays at one bound or the other and switches between them is **bang-bang control**
([indirect derivation](../../theory/methods/#example-minimum-time-move-and-stop)).

### Duration

Symmetry places the switch at $x=d/2$. From $a_{\max}t_1^2/2=d/2$,

$$
t_1=\sqrt{\frac{d}{a_{\max}}},\qquad t_f=2\sqrt{\frac{d}{a_{\max}}}=2\sqrt5.
$$

Peak velocity is $\sqrt{d\,a_{\max}}=\sqrt{20}$ m/s.

### Why split the phases?

A smooth polynomial cannot represent the acceleration jump accurately and oscillates near it.
With a knot at the switch, acceleration is constant in each phase and position and velocity remain continuous
([multiple phases](../../theory/pseudospectral/#multiple-phases)).

## Try it

- Set distance to 40. Arrival time doubles to $2\sqrt{40/2}\approx8.94$ s: quadrupling distance doubles time.
- Change the time guess from `Problem([0, 3, 6], ...)` to `Problem([0, 1, 6], ...)`. The solver may report success with a knot near 1.32 s, a switch spread across several nodes within phase 2, and duration about 4.50 s. This shows why knot placement matters.
- Call `solve(nodes=4)` instead of `solve()`. The phasewise polynomial solution remains accurate with fewer nodes.
